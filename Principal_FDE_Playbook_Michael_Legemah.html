part_1 · discovery → qualification → proposal

The AI upsell and expansion cheatsheet

Three phases, run in order. Most failed expansions skip the middle one: they go straight from "I noticed something" to "here's a proposal" without checking whether the idea deserves a customer's budget.

phase 1 · discovery

Spotting the signals

OpenClose

You already have the best discovery tool in the account: read access to their repos, their Jira and their runbooks. Code smells and operational friction tell you where AI pays off long before a stakeholder says the word "AI."

Code smells and friction checklist

0 of 8 signals spotted in this account

Five high-intent discovery questions

Ask these in casual syncs as often as formal ones. Each is built to surface a number, an owner or a blocker, which are the three things qualification needs.

  1. If you could delete one manual step from this workflow tomorrow, which would it be, and who does it today?Forces a specific step and a named team, which turns a vague wish into something you can time and cost. The "who" also tells you whose day gets better, which is your future champion.
  2. Where does work wait? Which queue has the oldest items, and what happens when something sits past SLA?Waiting is where cost hides in prior auth, claims and onboarding. SLA breaches usually carry a penalty, a regulatory clock or customer churn you can put a dollar figure on.
  3. What data do you wish you could ask questions of, but it's stuck in documents, transcripts or another team's system?Surfaces unstructured data and silos in one question. Listen for the word "another team's," because cross-team data is where governance work, and expansion scope, tends to sit.
  4. When this process gets something wrong, who finds out, how long does it take, and what does it cost you?Gives you the error rate, detection lag and cost per error, the three inputs to a risk-reduction ROI. In healthcare and banking the answer often involves a regulator or an audit finding, which raises urgency.
  5. What have you already tried with AI here, and what stopped it?Saves you from pitching what already failed and tells you the real blocker: data access, risk review, budget or results. Each blocker points to a different first step.
phase 2 · qualification & value mapping

Filtering "AI for the sake of AI" from real ROI

OpenClose

Every idea goes through five gates before it reaches a stakeholder. Ideas that pass get plotted on impact versus feasibility; ideas that fail get parked without regret, which is what keeps your credibility for the ones that matter.

The five-gate filter

Tick the gates the opportunity you're considering actually passes. Be strict: "probably" is a no.

0 of 5 gates

Park it. This is AI for the sake of AI until it clears at least four gates.

Red flags that mean "AI for the sake of AI"

  • Solution-first language

    "We need a chatbot" or "we need an agent" with no workflow attached. Ask what the chatbot would stop a person from doing; if nobody can answer, there's no use case yet.

  • Board-driven urgency

    "Leadership wants an AI announcement this quarter." Real, but it optimizes for a press release, not a metric. Steer it toward the quick win that will also make a good announcement.

  • No data owner

    Everyone agrees the data is valuable and nobody can approve access to it. Until a steward signs off, the project is a governance initiative, not an AI one.

  • A deterministic problem

    The task has exact rules and exact answers, like reconciling two ledgers. Traditional code is cheaper, faster and auditable; recommending it builds more trust than an LLM ever would.

The 2×2 prioritization strategy

Score each surviving idea 1 to 5 on both axes, then plot it (the matrix at the top of this page shows typical placements).

phase 3 · the soft pitch & proposal

Pitching as an embedded peer, not a vendor

OpenClose

The goal isn't to close anything. It's to make a small, specific, low-risk next step feel obvious to the person who owns the problem, then bring your account team in so commercial details never come from you.

Step by step

  1. Earn the observation

    Ship what you were brought in to ship. The pitch rides on delivered trust; raise it in the same week you closed a hard ticket, while your credibility is at its highest.

  2. Name the pain in their words, with their number

    "Your exception queue averages nine days" beats "AI can improve efficiency." Use their dashboard, their terminology and their units so there's nothing to argue with.

  3. Show, don't deck

    Build a 48-hour spike on synthetic or de-identified data in a sandbox. A working screen with ten real-looking examples beats a slide, and it answers the feasibility question before anyone asks it.

  4. Make it the champion's idea

    Walk the ops director or team lead through it privately first and let them present it upward. A champion who owns the idea fights for budget; one who was pitched at waits.

  5. Shrink the ask

    Propose a two- to four-week PoC with fixed scope, a single success metric agreed up front, explicit exit criteria and no production data until governance signs off. Small asks get approved without procurement drama.

  6. Hand off commercials transparently

    Tell the customer you're looping in your account lead for scoping and pricing, then do it. Your role stays technical, nobody is surprised, and your peer status survives the commercial conversation.

upsell_via_engineering · triggers, phrasing, patterns

Upselling via engineering

Expansion revenue that an engineer finds is different from expansion a salesperson pitches: it starts in a stack trace, a load test or a queue report, and it arrives with evidence. Here's how to notice it, say it, and pass it on without ever sounding like you're selling.

the pattern · observe → quantify → prototype → socialize → hand off

From signal to statement of work

OpenClose

Every organic expansion follows the same five moves. Skipping quantify makes you sound like an enthusiast; skipping hand off makes you sound like a salesperson.

Five sentence frames that keep you a peer

MoveSay it like thisWhy it works
Observe"While I was [fixing the intake parser], I noticed [the exception queue never shrinks]."Anchors the idea in work they asked you to do, so it reads as a by-product of diligence rather than an agenda.
Quantify"By my rough count that's [140 analyst hours] a week. Does that match what you see?"Uses their units and invites correction. Being corrected on a number is fine; it means they're now engaged with the problem.
Exit"I don't know if it's a priority for you right now."Removes pressure. Stakeholders lean in when they're given permission to say no.
Offer to show"Want me to show you what it does on [a dozen de-identified samples]?"An offer to show is an engineering request; an offer to sell is a commercial one. Only the first fits your role.
Hand off"I'll pull in [account lead] for scoping so I can stay on the build."Puts commercial talk where it belongs, openly. Your technical judgment stays uncontaminated by quota in the customer's eyes.
triggers · what you see → what you say → what it opens

Eight triggers and the exact words for each

OpenClose

Each trigger is something you'll run into in the normal course of engineering work. The quoted line is a starting script; swap in their numbers and their nouns.
Data silosopens: governed retrieval layer, new data connectors

You find the same entity (a member, a customer, a provider) joined across three or more systems through nightly CSV drops, and the copilot you deployed can only see one of them. Users ask it questions it structurally can't answer, and the logs show it.

The claims and member data never meet until the overnight batch, so the assistant can't answer "why was this denied" questions. I sketched how a governed retrieval layer could let it see both. Want me to show you which questions it would answer that it can't today?

Pull the unanswered-question log from your own deployment. It is the most persuasive evidence you will ever have because it's their users asking.

Scale limitsopens: provisioned throughput, higher tier, capacity planning

Your load test at peak volume (open enrollment, month-end close, tax season) shows 429 rate-limit errors or p95 latency climbing past what a live agent can tolerate. Today is fine; the calendar says it won't be.

We're comfortable at current volume, but I load-tested at open-enrollment levels and p95 goes to about 9 seconds. Worth talking about capacity in September rather than during November?

Framing it as risk avoidance on a known date makes the conversation urgent without any pressure from you.

Manual process bottlenecksopens: a second use case on the same platform

The workflow next to yours still runs on people reading documents and re-keying fields, and you can see the queue from where you sit. You've already built the extraction or retrieval piece that would cover most of it.

I timed the exception queue: about 14 minutes per item and 600 items a week, so roughly 140 analyst hours. I don't know if it's a priority, but the extraction we built for intake would cover most of those cases.

Reuse is the key word. The second use case costs a fraction of the first, which is exactly what makes it an easy approval.

Another team asks for your codeopens: enterprise-wide rollout, shared platform license

Fraud ops or care management asks if they can fork your repo or "borrow the pipeline." Forks drift, lose their guardrails and become ungoverned shadow deployments within a quarter.

Risk ops asked about forking our pipeline. Rather than a copy that drifts away from the guardrails, should we talk about making it a shared service both teams can use?

You're protecting governance, which compliance will back, and the natural answer is a wider deployment.

Evaluation and compliance gapsopens: continuous eval, monitoring, model governance module

Model risk management or a clinical safety committee starts asking how accuracy is measured, and the honest answer is a spreadsheet from launch week. Drift, prompt changes and model upgrades are all unmonitored.

Model risk will ask how we know this is still accurate in six months. I'd rather we have the answer before they ask. A continuous evaluation gate on every prompt and model change is a small add on top of what's there.

You're giving the customer a defense for an exam they know is coming. That is a gift, and it lands as one.

Shadow AIopens: governed enterprise AI gateway

DLP alerts, blocked-domain logs or plain conversation reveal staff pasting customer or patient data into consumer tools to get their work done.

I've seen a few teams paste data into public tools to get this done. That tells me the demand is real and so is the risk. A governed version would give security the audit trail they want and give those teams something approved.

Never name individuals. Present it as a pattern, and bring security in as a co-sponsor rather than an obstacle.

Cost creepopens: optimization work, budget freed for the next use case

Token spend has grown faster than usage because prompts got longer, retrieval returns too much context or every request hits the largest model.

Our token spend is up about 3x since launch, mostly from long prompts. Routing simple requests to a smaller model and caching repeat answers should cut that substantially, and it frees budget for the next workflow.

Proposing to lower their bill is the most counterintuitive and most trusted move on this list. It reliably turns into a "what should we do with the savings" conversation.

Success metric exceededopens: replicate the pattern across queues or lines of business

The PoC or first deployment beats its target ahead of schedule and the champion is visibly pleased.

We hit the 30% handle-time target in week six. Want to look at which other queues have the same shape as this one?

Ask in the same meeting as the good news. The moment of success is when the next scope is cheapest to agree on.

Lines you never cross
  • Never hold back a fix, slow a delivery or leave a limitation unaddressed to create a need. The moment a customer suspects it, every future recommendation is discounted.
  • Never quote price or negotiate. It changes how every technical opinion you give is heard, retroactively.
  • Never inflate a number. Give ranges, say "rough count," and show your math; a conservative estimate that's beaten is worth more than a big one that isn't.
  • Recommend the cheaper path, including no AI at all, when it's the right answer. Expansion only lasts when the customer is genuinely better off.

persona · the pragmatic software developer

Shipping under customer-site pressure

OpenClose

You're writing code in someone else's house, under their change control, with their compliance team watching. The rules below keep you fast without leaving a mess behind when you roll off.

Managing codebases under deployment pressure

  • Keep customer logic in adapters, never in the core

    Everything customer-specific (their EHR field mappings, their core banking API quirks) lives in a thin adapter layer that talks to the product through its public interfaces. When the next release ships, you upgrade the core and re-test adapters instead of hand-merging a fork.

  • Thin vertical slices behind feature flags

    Ship one end-to-end path (one document type, one queue, one user group) to production early, flagged off for everyone but pilot users. Change-advisory boards approve small, reversible changes far faster than big-bang releases.

  • Version prompts, models and retrieval config like code

    Prompts live in a registry with versions, model IDs are pinned, and every eval result is tied to the exact commit, prompt version and model version. When accuracy drops in week five, you can say exactly what changed instead of guessing.

  • An eval gate in CI from the first week

    A small golden set of 50 to 200 cases, built with the customer's subject-matter experts, runs on every merge. It catches regressions and becomes the evidence model risk and clinical safety reviewers ask for later.

Optimizing APIs on-site

  • Measure before touching anything

    Instrument p50, p95 and p99 per hop (retrieval, model call, tool calls, post-processing) with OpenTelemetry traces. The slow part is rarely where people assume, and the trace becomes your before-and-after evidence.

  • Parallelize and stream

    Run independent tool calls and retrievals concurrently, and stream tokens to the UI so the first useful words appear in under a second. Perceived latency matters as much as total latency for a live agent or nurse.

  • Cache and route

    Semantic caching for repeated questions, prompt caching for long static system prompts, and a router that sends simple classification work to a smaller model. These cut both latency and cost-to-serve.

  • Go async for long documents

    A 600-page loan file or a full medical record should be a queued job with idempotency keys and status polling, not a synchronous request that times out at the load balancer.

Building MVPs that stay stable

  • One workflow, real users, real (governed) data

    An MVP proves value in one workflow with real users. A demo on toy data proves nothing to a skeptical operations leader, and it hides the integration problems that will dominate the real project.

  • Human-in-the-loop by design, not as an afterthought

    Confidence thresholds route uncertain outputs to a review queue, and reviewer corrections feed the eval set. In regulated workflows this is what gets you through compliance review, and it gives you labeled data for free.

  • Leave a runbook and an owner

    Every MVP ships with a runbook (alerts, common failures, rollback steps) and a named customer engineer who has fixed at least one bug in it themselves. If it only works while you're on site, it isn't stable.

persona · the trusted business consultant

Pain, dollars and executive navigation

OpenClose

Technical metrics only matter to an executive once they become money, risk or time. The consultant's job is to make that translation credible, then put it in front of the right person in the right order.

The pain ladder

Climb one rung at a time in conversation. Stopping early is the most common mistake: a symptom isn't a business case, and a dollar figure without an owner doesn't get funded.

  1. Symptom

    What people complain about: "the queue is always behind," "we get the same question 50 times a day."

  2. Process

    Where in the workflow it happens, and which step creates it. Walk it with the person who does it; screenshare beats interviews.

  3. Metric

    The number that captures it today: backlog age, handle time, first-pass accuracy, denial overturn rate, write-offs.

  4. Dollars

    Labor cost, penalties, revenue at risk, regulatory exposure. Show your math and use their finance team's unit costs.

  5. Owner

    The executive accountable for that metric in their performance goals. That's your economic buyer, whether or not they're in your meetings yet.

Mapping technical metrics to ROI

OpenClose

Executives don't need to understand a context window. They need to know what it does to cost, risk and customers, and what it would take to fix. Lead with the consequence, then the plan, and keep the mechanism for whoever asks.

high-value habits for embedded engineers

Staying strategic while writing code

OpenClose

Deployment pressure pulls every FDE toward pure developer mode. These four habits take under three hours a week and keep the architect and consultant switched on.
daily · 10 minutesTicket-to-dollar note

At the end of each day, write one line per ticket you closed: which business metric it moved, or why it didn't move one. Keep it in a running doc.

After a month you have a ready-made impact story for the renewal review, and you'll notice which work is busywork worth pushing back on.

daily · 15 minutesRead one non-engineering artifact

An ops dashboard, an SLA report, a complaint log, a compliance memo or the customer's latest earnings call notes. Pick something your engineering peers never open.

This is where most expansion signals live, and it teaches you the customer's vocabulary so your proposals sound like they came from inside.

weekly · 30 minutesArchitecture redraw

Redraw the current topology from memory, then compare with last week's. Mark anything bespoke that should become a product feature, and any new coupling you introduced under pressure.

Bespoke code you flag this week is a product request; bespoke code you find in six months is tech debt the customer pays for.

weekly · 30 minutesSignal review with your account lead

Bring your signal log, run the strongest one or two through the five gates, and agree on at most one to raise that week.

One well-qualified idea a week beats five half-baked ones, and the rhythm keeps commercial follow-up with the account team instead of drifting to you.
